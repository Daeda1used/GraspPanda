    Read an analysis¶

    Goal: see where the AP of an evaluation comes from (which grasps earn it, which lose it, and why), and whether two results really differ.

    You need: a run of grasppanda test (Train and test). grasppanda analyze reads its result records and nothing else: no dataset, no weights, no predictions.

    1. Score a model¶

    The example on this page scores the authors' GraspNet-baseline checkpoint on every 64th frame of the seen test scenes, 120 frames:

    grasppanda test graspnet_baseline/graspnet-baseline_pointnet2_g1b-realsense_18e \
    --weights baseline_realsense.pth \
    --opts data.test.dataset.split=test_seen data.test.dataset.frame_step=64 \
    runtime.work_dir=work_dirs/baseline runtime.run_id=test_seen_step64 evaluation.num_workers=4

    Any method and any split work the same way: grasppanda test writes one result record per post-processing variant into <run>/results/.

    2. Analyze it¶

    grasppanda analyze work_dirs/baseline --variant cd --objects 5 --plot work_dirs/baseline_cd.png

    The report has a header, then the AP taken apart by friction threshold, by cause, by scene, by object and through the post-processing chain. --plot draws it (Pillow only).

    3. Read the header first¶

    The first block is everything a number needs before it may be quoted: protocol, split and camera, how many frames were scored, which inputs were derived from ground truth, the post-processing chain, and the weights and code that produced it. extent compares the frames of the record with the split it names. This run scored every 64th frame, so it is a subset, and the report says so in capitals: its AP of 0.4867 is a measurement on 120 frames, not the AP of GraspNet-baseline on the seen split.

    4. See where the precision comes from¶

    AP is the mean of a precision table with one column per friction threshold μ. The first table gives the mean of each column; their mean is the AP. Here a grasp that needs μ ≤ 0.2 is rare (0.18), and most of what can be earned is earned by μ ≤ 0.8.

    The second table attributes the AP. A frame's AP is a weighted sum over its 50 ranks: rank 1 owns about 9 % of it, rank 50 owns 0.04 %. Every rank either earns its share, or loses it for exactly one reason: the gripper collides with the scene (checked against the ground-truth models), nothing is between the fingers, the grasp is free but not force-closed at this μ, or the rank was never filled. The columns add up to one. In this run 29 % of the AP is lost to collisions although the model-free collision filter ran.

    By object, by scene, and through the post-processing chain

    By object applies the same split to the object each ranked grasp belongs to. The numbers are percentage points of AP: object 0 earns 3.07 points and loses 4.99 to collisions, more than any other object. success is the share an object's ranks earned of what they held. Object ids are the dataset's.

    By scene lists the scenes at both ends. Scenes, not frames, are the unit to think in: the four frames of a scene show the same objects.

    Grasps follows the predictions through the post-processing chain. For a chain with two steps each step has its own row. HGGD's cd variant on the same frames:

    The split by cause and object and the per-step counts are stored in the record by grasppanda test (fields breakdown and grasp_counts). Records written before these fields existed are read as before; the report then gives the total the chain removed, from the grasp counts of the run's records, and says that the rest is not recorded.

    5. Compare two results¶

    grasppanda analyze --compare work_dirs/baseline:cd work_dirs/sbg:cd --scenes 5 \
    --plot work_dirs/baseline_vs_sbg.png

    A:cd picks a variant of a run; --variant picks one for both. The two records must share protocol and dataset. Whatever else differs between them (split, cameras, ground-truth-assisted inputs, post-processing, weights, code) is listed under differs, so that a difference in the numbers is not credited to the wrong cause. The summary pairs scenes: a bootstrap interval of the mean difference and a sign test, both with a scene as one observation.
